Understanding Homelessness: A Research Framework

Benjamin F. Henwood, PhD, MSW
Mapping what we know, what is emerging, and where the evidence gaps are—across the full spectrum of homelessness research.
Evidence Status: Strong evidence Emerging evidence Evidence gap

Homelessness is poorly understood not because the evidence is lacking, but because the evidence is fragmented. This framework integrates research across four causal layers, an empirical domain, and cross-cutting prevention—connecting structural causes to individual outcomes. Scale of the Challenge

52,000+
People unsheltered in LA County on any given night — the largest unsheltered population in the US

LA has some of the highest rents and lowest housing vacancy rates nationally, a history of racially discriminatory housing policy, and one of the widest wage-to-housing-cost gaps in the country.

Racial Disparity

8% → 30%+
Black residents: 8% of county population, 30%+ of homeless population

This disparity is not incidental. It is the direct expression of structural conditions—redlining, discriminatory lending, over-policing, and barriers to employment and wealth accumulation.

2025 Point-in-Time Count

2.8%↓
CA decline in homelessness (2024–2025); first national decline since 2016

California officials attribute decline to Homekey, HHAP, and Encampment Resolution Funds. HUD's new NOFO moves away from Housing First despite this evidence. 19 states including CA are suing.

Measure A / Evaluation Agenda

LA County's 2024 Measure A created a dedicated homelessness funding stream and 16-priority Annual Evaluation Agenda. It covers Layer 4 (formal response system) in depth. But it leaves significant gaps in the full framework.

Evaluation Infrastructure

What Current Systems Cover — and Don't

Measure A/AEA and LACAHSA represent significant advances but do not cover the full framework.
✓

Covered: Formal Response System (Layer 4)

Measure A/AEA evaluates systemwide impact, racial disparities in housing stability, coordinated entry, outreach engagement, and workforce conditions. Deep coverage of the formal homeless services system.

✓

Covered: Housing Supply (Layer 2, partial)

LACAHSA addresses affordable housing production, eviction diversion, and affordable stock preservation—operating in structural conditions and targeted prevention domains.

~

Partial: Pathways (Layer 3)

Some attention to referral pathways from foster care, criminal justice, and behavioral health through coordinated entry evaluations—but preventing these systems from producing homelessness requires policy change outside homelessness funding streams.

✗

Not Covered: Structural Conditions (Layer 2)

Racial and economic inequality, wage stagnation, macro-level policy failures are largely outside both Measure A and LACAHSA scope. No evaluation of housing supply dynamics or structural drivers of racial disparity at macro level.

✗

Not Covered: Adjacent Institutional Systems

Healthcare, criminal justice, and schools shape homelessness trajectories but are not funded through homelessness-specific streams and rarely included in official evaluations.

✗

Not Covered: Community Response

Mutual aid, faith organizations, and peer support are largely invisible to official evaluations—not captured in HMIS, rarely included in RFP processes. The faith-based sector in particular has significant land assets and community trust that formal systems cannot replicate.

✗

Not Covered: Public Attitudes (Layer 1)

No current evaluation effort addresses public narratives, community trust, or political will—despite being the foundational condition on which all other investments depend.

✗

Not Covered: Prevention (structural level)

Prevention remains significantly underfunded relative to its ROI, at both the structural (affordable housing, income support) and targeted (eviction diversion, rapid rehousing) levels.

Evidence Gaps

Where the Research is Most Needed

These are the highest-priority areas where current evidence is insufficient to guide policy—and where HPRI is positioned to contribute.
Critical Gap · Layer 1

Public Attitudes and Political Will

The foundational layer of the framework receives almost no systematic research attention. We lack rigorous evidence on what messaging strategies effectively shift public opinion toward systemic framing, how attitudes drive policy investment decisions, or how to build durable political coalitions for sustained homelessness investment.

This is not merely an academic gap. The current federal retreat from Housing First is driven partly by political framing, not evidence. Understanding and countering that framing requires its own evidence base.

HPRI opportunity: Research on narrative and framing effects, public opinion polling, and the political economy of homelessness investment. Connects directly to Layer 1-Layer 2 feedback loop.
Critical Gap · Empirical Domain

Population-Representative Data on the Unsheltered

Point-in-time counts are methodologically limited, particularly for unsheltered populations. Longitudinal, population-representative data on health trajectories, housing trajectories, and social connection during homelessness is extremely rare.

PATHS (Periodic Assessment of Trajectories of Housing, Health, and Homelessness) is one of very few such studies nationally. Expanding and sustaining this kind of data infrastructure is essential for everything else in this framework.

HPRI contribution: PATHS study directly addresses this gap. HUD NOFO (Topic G) application would further advance alternative enumeration methods.
Critical Gap · Outcomes

Whether Interventions Are Reducing Racial Disparities

Racial disparities in homelessness entry are well-documented, but we know very little about whether current interventions differentially benefit or fail Black, AIAN, and other overrepresented groups. Racially disaggregated outcome data is improving but still limited.

HPRI contribution: CAIS/CESTTRR partnership (Eric Rice, USC) advances race equity measurement. PATHS provides racially disaggregated longitudinal data.
Critical Gap · Layer 4

Community-Based and Faith-Based Response

Mutual aid networks, faith organizations, and peer support reach people and build trust in ways formal institutions cannot—often ahead of the formal system. Yet this component is almost entirely absent from HMIS, excluded from RFP processes, and rarely evaluated.

Faith-based organizations in particular hold significant land assets and long-term community relationships that cannot be replicated by government systems. The evidence base for their role and effectiveness is nearly nonexistent.

HPRI contribution: USC Housing LA Faith-Based Research Initiative (Seth Pickens/HPRI; Hyepin Im/FACE; NDSC, CRCC partners) is directly building this evidence base—mapping faith-based land assets and community engagement capacity across LA.
Critical Gap · Layer 4

Workforce Conditions and Provider Stability

High turnover, low wages, and inadequate support among frontline providers are recognized but almost entirely unevaluated. Frontline workers are often from the same communities as clients; some are themselves at homelessness risk. Workforce instability is both an equity issue and a service quality issue.

HPRI opportunity: Measure A's AEA includes workforce as an evaluation priority—creating an opening for HPRI to contribute rigorous research in this understudied area.
Critical Gap · Layer 3

Scale and Adequacy of Investment

Research consistently shows that homelessness declines when investment is adequate—and rises when it isn't. Yet the field lacks a clear, evidence-based benchmark for what "adequate investment" looks like per person experiencing homelessness. Without this, debates about funding remain politically driven rather than evidence-based.

LA currently invests approximately $17K per person experiencing homelessness annually; evidence suggests $40K+ is required for durable housing stability. The gap between current and adequate investment is itself a research and policy question.

HPRI contribution: "You Get What You Pay For" policy brief establishes the adequacy framework. Transparency about what current investment levels can realistically achieve—and what they cannot—is a distinctive HPRI contribution to the field.
