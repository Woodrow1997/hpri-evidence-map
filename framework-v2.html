HPRI Integrative Research Framework

Homelessness is not one problem.
It is many forces, all interacting.

Six analytical levels, four cross-cutting forces, one feedback loop. A multi-level analytical framework mapping the structural, institutional, and political determinants of homelessness in the U.S. urban context.

Benjamin F. Henwood, PhD, MSW · USC Homelessness Policy Research Institute · Scope: U.S. urban context; applicability to rural or non-U.S. settings requires validation, PhD USC HPRI v2 · June 2026

Homelessness is poorly understood not because the evidence is lacking, but because the evidence is fragmented. No common framework connects it. The result is a public conversation dominated by oversimplification — and policy responses that treat symptoms instead of causes.

Benjamin F. Henwood, PhD · Director, USC HPRI
How to read this framework
Vertical Layers
Six analytical levels run top to bottom — from political determinants and structural conditions, through proximate pathways into homelessness, to institutional response and outcomes. These are not a causal chain — they are interacting levels of influence.
Horizontal Forces
Four forces — Race, History, Place, and Climate — cut across every layer simultaneously, shaping who is vulnerable, who gets served, and who gets housed.
The Feedback Loop
Outcomes don't just measure success — they feed back into public opinion and political power, shaping everything above them. Research that documents outcomes is itself a policy intervention.
The Framework

Six Layers. One System.

Each layer represents a distinct domain of causes, conditions, and responses. Together, they trace the full arc from structural vulnerability to individual outcome.

Layer 01 · Political Forces
1A
Public Opinion & Narrative
What do citizens believe about homelessness?
Public attitudes — shaped by media framing, direct exposure, and moral beliefs about personal responsibility — set the political ceiling for what is possible. When the public understands homelessness as a systemic failure, they support sustained investment. When they understand it as a personal failing, they support sweeps and enforcement.
⚠ Evidence gap: attitudes research is significantly underrepresented in the literature
1B
Political Economy & Organized Power
Who actually controls homelessness policy?
Public opinion and political outcomes frequently diverge. Real estate lobbies, corrections interests, and well-funded think tanks — most notably the Cicero Institute, which has shaped 650+ pieces of legislation since 2021 — form the organized power infrastructure that defines what politicians treat as the legitimate policy menu, independent of what evidence or public sympathy suggests.
⚑ A sympathetic public + a hostile power structure = criminalization
↕ The gap between Layers 1A and 1B is where policy is actually made. Understanding this gap is essential to understanding why evidence-based approaches are not simply adopted once the evidence exists.
mutually reinforcing
02
Layer 02 · Root Causes
What makes millions of people one crisis away from losing their home?
A shortage of 7.2 million affordable homes, compounded by racial and economic inequality, stagnant wages, and the progressive financialization of housing — the transformation of homes into investment vehicles — create widespread vulnerability before a single individual makes a single choice. These are the conditions that set the floor. Universal prevention begins by addressing them directly.
Housing scarcity Racial inequity Wage stagnation Financialization
Key Finding
Colburn & Aldern (2022) demonstrate that housing market conditions — vacancy rates and rent levels — are stronger predictors of homelessness rates than mental illness, drug use, or weather. Los Angeles ranks among the worst in the nation on both measures.
Evidence Status
Housing scarcity and racial inequity are well-established drivers. Financialization of housing as a structural mechanism is emerging as a distinct research area.
03
Layer 03 · Pathways In
Where does structural vulnerability become an individual crisis?
Vulnerability becomes homelessness through predictable institutional channels. Youth who age out of foster care face some of the highest homelessness risk of any population. People released from incarceration encounter compounding barriers — criminal records, housing bars, abrupt transitions. Behavioral health system failures discharge people without adequate support. Discrimination in housing and employment converts vulnerability to crisis for those who face it. These are not individual failures. They are the predictable outputs of institutional systems with design flaws we know how to fix.
Foster care exits Incarceration Behavioral health Discrimination
Key Finding
Youth aging out of foster care face lifetime homelessness risk of 30–40%. Each additional institutional system involvement compounds risk multiplicatively, not additively.
Empirical core & cross-cutting strategy
Empirical Domain
The Experience of Homelessness
Situated between the pathways that produce homelessness and the systems that respond to it is a domain that must be understood on its own terms: who actually experiences homelessness, what happens to their health, how they navigate or avoid services, and how their trajectories unfold over time. Individual agency, resilience, and social networks shape how people navigate homelessness — and this framework does not erase them. It argues instead that structural and institutional factors are the most consistently under-weighted in policy discourse. Without this knowledge, we design systems for people we don't actually know.
HPRI's PATHS Study — Periodic Assessment of Trajectories of Housing, Homelessness, and Health — is one of the only longitudinal, population-representative studies of unsheltered adults in the country. It documents what administrative systems cannot see.

Cross-Cutting Strategy
Prevention
Prevention isn't one program — it's a principle that operates at every level of the framework. Upstream: housing supply expansion and income supports. Midstream: eviction diversion and emergency rental assistance. Downstream: rapid rehousing before homelessness becomes chronic.
Research consistently shows preventing homelessness is substantially less expensive than addressing it after the fact. Prevention is also the most underfunded tool we have.
Consistently underfunded
04
Layer 04 · How We Respond
What does the response system look like — and what is it missing?
Three components operate in parallel. The formal homeless services system — outreach, coordinated entry, interim housing, permanent housing, and stability supports — is most visible to policymakers and most systematically evaluated. Adjacent institutional systems — healthcare, criminal justice, schools — engage with unhoused people through different funding streams and accountability structures. Community-based response — mutual aid, faith organizations, peer support workers — reaches people and builds trust in ways institutions cannot. This third component is largely invisible to official data and almost never evaluated. HPRI's USC Housing LA Faith-Based Initiative is directly addressing this gap.
Housing First Street outreach Coordinated entry Faith & community Peer workforce
Evidence Status
Housing First: strong evidence base across populations and settings. Coordinated entry: emerging. Faith and community organizations: significant evidence gap — most effective community-based response is systematically underevaluated.
05
Layer 05 · What Success Looks Like
What are we trying to achieve — for individuals, and for everyone?
Successful response produces two categories of outcomes that are equally important and too rarely connected. Individual outcomes — housing stability, health recovery, agency, and dignity — are the most direct measure. Community outcomes — safer public spaces, reduced emergency costs, stronger neighborhoods, reduced racial inequity — represent the broader social value of ending homelessness. Framing homelessness as a public investment with returns for everyone, not just for a vulnerable minority, is essential to building the durable political will that Layer 1 requires.
Housing stability Health recovery Community benefit Racial equity
Evidence Gap
Whether racial disparities in housing outcomes are narrowing is one of the most significant and least studied questions in the field. Strong evidence of disparity; weak evidence on whether any current interventions are closing it.
↺
The Feedback Loop
Visible Outcomes → Media Framing → Public Opinion → Political Pressure → Policy
Three mechanisms drive this feedback: (1) visible street homelessness shapes media coverage, which shapes public attitudes toward investment vs. enforcement; (2) documented program outcomes shift the political calculus for elected officials; (3) research — including HPRI's — is itself a feedback mechanism, re-introducing evidence into the political layer. Closing this loop is one of the most important things a research institute can do. When homelessness visibly worsens, public stigma and punitive responses intensify — feeding back into Layer 1 and foreclosing investment. When programs work and outcomes are visible, public support grows and the political cost of investment falls. Research that clearly documents outcomes is therefore not just measurement — it is itself an intervention in the political dynamics that govern every other layer of the framework.
Cross-Cutting Forces
Four forces that shape every layer

These are not variables to control for — they are structural conditions that simultaneously shape every layer of the framework, from who is vulnerable to who gets housed.

Primary Force
◉
Race & Racism
Race shapes every layer. Black Americans are 3–4× more likely to experience homelessness than white Americans — a disparity reflecting not individual behavior but the accumulated weight of discriminatory housing policy, unequal wealth, and institutional bias operating across generations. In LA, Black residents are 8% of the population and over 30% of the homeless population. An equity-blind response system reproduces these disparities even when it appears neutral.
⟳
History & Time
Modern mass homelessness in the US emerged from specific policy choices made between 1970 and 1990: an 80% cut in federal housing investment under Reagan, the failure of deinstitutionalization to provide community support, and labor market restructuring that eroded wages. Cumulative disadvantage compounds across the life course — and across generations. Understanding the historical arc reveals that the causes have been building for fifty years, and the solutions will require the same horizon.
◎
Place & Geography
Where you live shapes whether you become homeless and whether you get housed. Housing affordability, spatial concentration of poverty, NIMBYism as localized political economy, transit access, and residential segregation are not peripheral details — they are central mechanisms. Local opposition to affordable housing, operating through zoning codes and planning commissions, constitutes one of the most persistent structural barriers to scale in the entire system.
△
Climate & Displacement
Climate change is no longer background noise for homelessness research — it is a direct cause and an accelerant of every other layer. The January 2025 LA wildfires displaced 100,000+ people into a housing market with no slack. Extreme heat kills unhoused people at up to 200× the housed rate. Climate disasters preferentially destroy lower-value housing stock — exactly the affordable supply that people trying to exit homelessness need. The pipeline from climate disruption to homelessness is getting shorter.
