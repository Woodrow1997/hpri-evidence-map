Step 1 of 10 · Where it all starts

"In a democracy, who decides
how society responds to
social problems?"

In theory, policy should reflect what people want. In a democracy, public opinion matters — what we collectively believe about who deserves help, whether housing is a right, and what government should do shapes the laws and programs we create. But politics and power also matter: lobbyists, economic interests, and narratives shaped by powerful groups all influence what gets built, funded, and prioritized.

🗣️
Public Opinion & Values
What do people believe? Should housing be a right? Who do we take care of as a society? These questions — answered through polls, elections, and culture — are supposed to drive policy. Research and data should inform public understanding, but so do narratives and media framing.
⚖️
Politics & Power
Who actually shapes policy? Lobbyists, real estate interests, and advocacy groups all compete to influence government decisions. Power doesn’t always follow public opinion — which is why evidence alone rarely changes policy without political will behind it.
↔ What the public wants and what powerful interests push for are often not the same thing — and that gap shapes everything downstream.
Why start here? Every program, law, and funding decision flows from this layer. Research and data should inform both public understanding and policy — but narratives shaped by power interests often get there first. Understanding this dynamic is the foundation for understanding everything else in the framework.
Step 2 of 10 · Root causes

"Why is homelessness
even possible at scale?"

Before anyone loses their home, the conditions for homelessness are already built into the system. Millions of people are put in a position where a single crisis — a job loss, a health emergency, a breakup — is all it takes to lose housing.

🏗️
Framework Layer 2 · Root Causes
Structural Conditions
A national shortage of 7.2 million affordable homes
Wages that haven't kept pace with housing costs
Racial and economic inequality concentrating vulnerability
Financialization — homes treated as investments, not shelter
The evidence is clear: Cities with tighter housing markets have higher rates of homelessness — not cities with more mental illness or drug use. Los Angeles has among the worst housing market conditions in the country. This layer is where universal prevention happens: housing supply, income support, and reducing inequality.
Step 3 of 10 · The breaking point

"Why do people become
homeless in the first place?"

Structural conditions create vulnerability. But vulnerability becomes homelessness through specific events — often predictable failures of institutions that were supposed to help. These are not individual failures. They are design flaws.

🌊
Framework Layer 3 · The Breaking Point
Pathways into Homelessness
Youth aging out of foster care with no housing or support
Release from incarceration with no stable place to go
Behavioral health system failures — discharged without support
Discrimination in housing and employment blocking reentry
Think of it this way: The system puts people in precarious positions (Layer 2), then specific institutions — foster care, prisons, psychiatric hospitals — discharge people into crisis instead of into stability. Reforming these pathways is a form of targeted prevention with some of the best evidence in the field.
Step 4 of 10 · The lived reality

"What is it actually like
to be homeless?"

Research can trace causes and measure programs — but if we don't actually know who's experiencing homelessness and what's happening to them, we end up designing solutions for people we don't really understand.

🔬
Empirical Domain · Making the Invisible Visible
The Experience of Homelessness
Health trajectories — homelessness accelerates aging and illness
Housing cycling — people move in and out, rarely in a straight line
Who connects to services — and who the system completely misses
HPRI's PATHS study is one of the only longitudinal, population-representative studies of unsheltered people in the country. It follows real people over time to document trajectories that administrative data — which only captures people when they interact with systems — simply cannot see. Without this layer, we build systems for an abstraction.
Step 5 of 10 · The most underfunded tool

"How do we stop people
from falling in?"

Prevention isn't a single program — it's a principle that runs through every layer of this framework. It's also consistently the most cost-effective investment available. And it's chronically underfunded.

🛡️
Cross-Cutting Strategy · Operates at Every Level
Prevention
Upstream: housing supply, income supports, reducing inequality
Midstream: eviction diversion and emergency rental assistance
Downstream: rapid rehousing before homelessness becomes chronic
The math is simple: Preventing homelessness is substantially less expensive than addressing it after it becomes chronic. A night in emergency shelter costs far more than a month of rental assistance that prevents homelessness entirely. The consistent underfunding of prevention — in favor of emergency response — is one of the most correctable failures in the current system.
Step 6 of 10 · The response system

"What happens when
systems try to help?"

Once someone is homeless, two overlapping worlds respond: the formal system most people think of — outreach, shelters, housing programs — and the informal community networks that often reach people the formal system never does.

🏥
Framework Layer 4 · How We Respond
Institutional & Community Response
Outreach, coordinated entry, interim housing, permanent housing
Adjacent systems: hospitals, courts, schools (often overlooked)
Faith orgs, mutual aid, peer workers — the half that never gets evaluated
Housing First — the evidence-based model that prioritizes getting people into stable housing before addressing other issues — has the strongest evidence base in this layer. But the formal system is only half the ecosystem. HPRI's USC Housing LA Faith-Based Initiative is directly measuring what faith and community organizations contribute, because they're reaching people institutions cannot.
Step 7 of 10 · What success looks like

"What does success
actually look like?"

Success isn't just about getting someone housed — though that's the foundation. When homelessness is addressed well, it produces benefits for both the individual and for the broader community. This framing matters politically.

🏠
Outcomes · For People and Communities
Individual & Community Outcomes
Individual: housing stability, health recovery, dignity, economic participation
Community: safer public spaces, reduced emergency costs, stronger neighborhoods
Systemic: reduced racial inequity, improved long-term economic outcomes
Addressing homelessness is a public investment with community-wide returns — not charity for a few. Framing it this way is both accurate and essential for building the political will (back in Layer 1) that makes sustained investment possible. Racial equity outcomes remain among the most important and least-evaluated in the field.
Step 8 of 10 · The loop

"How do outcomes shape
politics and power?"

This framework isn't a one-way pipeline from politics to outcomes. What happens at the end feeds back to the beginning — shaping what the public believes and what politicians feel they must do. The loop runs in both directions.

🔄
Framework Insight · The Feedback Loop
Outcomes Shape Power and Opinion
Visible worsening → increased public stigma → punitive policy responses
Visible program success → stronger public support → more investment
Research documenting outcomes is itself a political intervention
This is why research communication matters. When programs work but no one documents it clearly, political support erodes. When homelessness visibly worsens — even for reasons outside local control — punitive responses intensify, further harming the people experiencing homelessness and setting back the system. The feedback loop runs both toward improvement and toward collapse. Which direction it runs depends partly on what gets measured, documented, and communicated.
Step 9 of 10 · The forces behind everything

"What makes everything
harder or easier?"

Three forces don't fit neatly into a single layer — they cut across the entire framework, shaping every step of the causal chain. Ignore them and any analysis of homelessness is incomplete.

⏳
History & Time
Homelessness is a historical product. Federal housing disinvestment starting in 1978, deinstitutionalization, wage erosion — decisions made 50 years ago explain why structural conditions are where they are. Cumulative disadvantage compounds: each past hardship amplifies the next.
📍
Place & Geography
Where you live determines whether homelessness is likely and whether exit is possible. Zoning codes, NIMBY opposition to affordable housing, residential segregation, and transit access shape who becomes homeless and who gets housed.
🌡️
Climate & Displacement
Wildfires, flooding, and extreme heat are now direct causes of homelessness — and they accelerate every other layer. The 2025 LA wildfires displaced 100,000+ people into an already-impossible housing market. Unhoused people face up to 200× higher heat mortality risk.
These three forces explain why homelessness looks different in different cities, why it's worsening now, and why solutions that worked elsewhere may not work in LA. A framework that ignores history, place, and climate is a framework that can't explain what's actually happening.
You've seen the whole picture

Solutions come from
every layer — at once.

There's no single lever that ends homelessness. The framework shows why: root causes, broken pathways, inadequate responses, and political forces all interact. So do the solutions — housing supply, pathway reform, evidence-based response, community investment, and narrative change all need to move together.

HPRI exists to build the research infrastructure that makes that possible.
