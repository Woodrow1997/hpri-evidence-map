HPRI Integrative Research Framework — v2.0

Homelessness is not one problem.
It is many forces, all interacting.

Vertical forces drive people toward and out of homelessness. Horizontal forces — history, place, and climate — shape every one of them. This framework shows how they connect.

"Homelessness is poorly understood not because the evidence is lacking, but because the evidence is fragmented. No common framework connects it. The result is a public conversation dominated by oversimplification — and policy responses that treat symptoms instead of causes." — Benjamin F. Henwood, PhD · HPRI

Layer 1A · Public Opinion & Values
🗣️
Public Opinion & Narrative
What citizens believe about homelessness — stigma, framing effects, media coverage, and moral attributions — shapes what politicians can do and what they feel they must do.
Evidence gap: attitudes research is scarce

Political & Economic Power
Who actually controls policy: real estate lobbies, corrections interests, think tanks like the Cicero Institute, developer coalitions. Often moves in the opposite direction from public opinion.
Sympathetic public + hostile power = criminalization

Layer 2 · Distal Root Causes
Structural Conditions
A shortage of 7.2 million affordable homes, racial and economic inequality, stagnant wages, and the financialization of housing create vulnerability. These forces make millions of people one crisis away from losing housing. Universal prevention begins here.
✓ Housing scarcity
✓ Racial inequity
✓ Wage stagnation
~ Financialization
🌊
Layer 3 · The Breaking Point
Proximate Pathways into Homelessness
Vulnerability becomes homelessness through specific pathways — and those pathways are not random. Individual circumstances matter: mental illness, substance use, divorce, job loss. But whether those circumstances lead to homelessness depends on what structures and systems are in place to catch people. Individual agency and resilience shape trajectories, but they do not eliminate risk created by institutional design failures: foster care discharge, incarceration without housing, behavioral health system gaps, and discrimination that blocks reentry.
✓ Foster care exits
✓ Incarceration
✓ Behavioral health
~ Discrimination
🔬
Empirical Domain · The Terrain Research Must Make Visible
The Experience of Homelessness
Who actually experiences homelessness — and what happens to them? Health trajectories, aging acceleration, service navigation, social isolation. HPRI's PATHS study is one of the only longitudinal datasets on unsheltered people in the country. Without this layer, we design systems for people we don't actually know.
🛡️
Cross-Cutting · Operates at Every Level
Prevention
Prevention isn't a single program — it's a principle operating across layers. Upstream: housing supply and income. Midstream: eviction diversion. Downstream: rapid rehousing. Consistently the most cost-effective investment. Chronically underfunded.
🏥
Layer 4 · How We Respond
Institutional & Community Response
Outreach, shelters, coordinated entry, permanent housing, street medicine, hospitals, courts — and faith organizations, mutual aid, and peer workers who reach people institutions cannot. The formal system and community response are two halves of one ecosystem, but only one gets evaluated.

Outcomes · For People and Communities
Individual & Community Outcomes
Success means housing stability, health recovery, dignity, and economic participation — for individuals. And safer public spaces, reduced emergency costs, stronger neighborhoods, and reduced racial inequity — for everyone. Addressing homelessness is a public investment with community-wide returns.

The Feedback Loop: Visible Outcomes → Media Framing → Public Opinion → Political Pressure → Policy

Outcomes don't feed back abstractly — they do so through specific mechanisms: (1) visible street homelessness shapes media coverage, which shapes public attitudes; (2) evidence of program success (or failure) shifts the political calculus for elected officials; (3) research and data — including HPRI's — is itself a feedback mechanism, re-introducing evidence into the political layer. This reflexive role of knowledge production is part of what makes the framework more than descriptive.

Four Forces That Cut Across Every Layer — Click to Explore

These forces are distinguished from structural conditions in that they shape the distribution of homelessness and the quality of response across all layers — not just who is at risk, but who falls, who suffers, and who gets helped.

⬤

Race & Racism

Race shapes every layer of this framework — who is vulnerable, who falls into homelessness, who gets served, and who gets housed. Black Americans are 3–4× more likely to experience homelessness than white Americans. This reflects the accumulated result of discriminatory housing policy, unequal wealth, and biased institutions operating across generations.

⏳

History & Time

Cumulative disadvantage compounds. The arc from 1978 federal disinvestment to today explains why structural conditions are where they are. Homelessness is a historical product, not a timeless social problem.

📍

Place & Geography

Spatial concentration, neighborhood effects, NIMBYism as localized political economy, transit access, and residential segregation shape who experiences homelessness and who gets housed.

🌡️

Climate & Displacement

Wildfires, heat, and flooding are increasingly producing homelessness directly — and accelerating every other layer. The Palisades fire alone displaced thousands into a housing market with no slack.
