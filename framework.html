HPRI Research Framework

Homelessness is not one problem.
It is four interacting forces.

To understand — and end — homelessness, we need a framework that connects structural causes, lived experience, and system responses. This is ours.

"Homelessness is poorly understood not because the evidence is lacking, but because the evidence is fragmented. No common framework connects it. The result is a public conversation dominated by oversimplification." — Benjamin F. Henwood, PhD · HPRI

The Framework

🗣️
Layer 1 · The Foundation of All Policy
Public Attitudes & Political Will
Every investment in housing, services, or prevention ultimately depends on political will — which is shaped by public attitudes. When communities understand homelessness as a systemic failure, they support sustained investment. When they see it as individual failure, they fund sweeps.

Layer 2 · Root Causes
Structural Conditions
Racial inequality, a shortage of 7.2 million affordable homes, stagnant wages, and decades of policy failure create vulnerability. These forces don't cause homelessness directly — they make people one crisis away from losing housing. Universal prevention begins here.

Layer 3 · The Breaking Point
Pathways into Homelessness
Vulnerability becomes homelessness through specific pathways: the foster care system discharges young people with nowhere to go; incarceration leaves people with no housing or income; the behavioral health system has never fully replaced institutions it shut down. Discrimination and isolation do the rest.

Empirical Domain · The Terrain Research Must Make Visible
The Experience of Homelessness
Who actually experiences homelessness? How does it affect health, aging, and housing over time? How do people navigate services — and who falls through every gap? This is the evidence layer that makes everything else legible. Without it, we're designing systems for people we don't actually know. HPRI's PATHS study is one of the only longitudinal datasets on unsheltered people in the country.
🛡️
Cross-Cutting · Operates at Every Level
Prevention
Prevention isn't a single program — it's a principle. Upstream: build affordable housing, raise wages, reduce inequality. Midstream: eviction diversion, emergency rental assistance. Downstream: rapid rehousing before crisis becomes chronic. Prevention is consistently the most cost-effective investment available and the most chronically underfunded.
🏥
Layer 4 · How We Respond
Institutional & Community Response
Outreach, shelters, coordinated entry, permanent housing, street medicine, hospitals, courts, and schools all engage people experiencing homelessness. So do faith organizations, mutual aid networks, and peer workers — often reaching people that institutions cannot. The formal system and the community response are not separate: they are two halves of one ecosystem.

Outcomes · For People and Communities
Individual & Community Outcomes
Success means housing stability, health recovery, dignity, and economic participation — for individuals. But it also means safer public spaces, reduced emergency costs, stronger neighborhoods, and reduced racial inequity — for everyone. Addressing homelessness isn't charity. It's a public investment with returns for the whole community.

The Feedback Loop: Outcomes Shape Attitudes

When homelessness visibly worsens, stigma intensifies. When programs work — when communities see former neighbors stably housed, when costs fall, when public spaces improve — support for investment grows. Research that documents these outcomes clearly and accessibly is itself an intervention in the political layer that governs everything else.
